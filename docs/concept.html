§ 01 · Prologue

A visualizer that learned to breathe

Efflorescent started as an ambient screen for an Android app that ran local AI models. The job was to show how hard the phone was working without printing a single number. The answer was an ecosystem.

In Bioluminescent Dreamscape, creatures swam through dark water. When the model was thinking, the water got busier: more swimmers, faster strokes, richer colors, bursts of caustic light. Heat made the creatures swim erratically and shift toward red. A dying battery dimmed everything to a cold, starved blue. You could tell the state of the device from across the room, just by looking.

That is already most of what a strategy game needs from its presentation layer. State is carried by living things, and you read it at a glance. Each creature was also assembled from interchangeable organs (legs, flagella, pili, mandibles, antennae), and each organ scales through ten variants. Put those two facts together and it sounds a lot like Spore.

So the pitch is this: keep the visual language and let it drive a simulation. Instead of watching a phone think, you tend a culture. You harvest light, evolve organs along biological tech trees, build more complex organisms, and send them into the drift against rival cultures.

“The screen was never showing us a phone. It was showing us an ecosystem that happened to be reporting on one. Efflorescent lets that ecosystem report on itself.”
High conceptA real-time strategy game of bioluminescent evolution. Six glowing cultures compete for drifting pools of light in an endless abyss. Every organ you evolve changes how your creatures look and what they can do, and your whole colony's health shows up in its color.

Design pillars

I

Legible Light

You can read the state of the game from the scene itself. Color shows your colony's condition, motion shows its intensity, and brightness shows health. The HUD is there to back that up; you shouldn't need it.

II

Anatomy is Strategy

No abstract upgrades. Every piece of research grows a visible organ, so you can size up an enemy army by looking at its silhouette.

III

Living Terrain

Resource pools drift. Front lines move like tides, which means territory is something you keep tending, not something you capture once.

IV

Push, then Burn

Fever is a shared lever. Overdrive your culture and it gets faster and deadlier, then it starts consuming itself.

§ 03 · The Dreamscape

An ocean without walls

The Dreamscape is a lightless sea where the only light is alive. The seed paints it in stacked layers: abyss gradient, surface shimmer, caustics, dust, swimmers and plankton. In the game, each layer becomes a depth band and each light source becomes something to fight over.

The Shallows

Surface shimmer · photic band

The pale, rippling top of the world. Light arrives here unowned. In the full game, the Shallows are where new Lumen pools condense and drift down, so whoever controls the upper water controls future income.

The Drift

Caustic layer · the contested middle

Where the caustic pools wander. This is where nearly all fighting happens. Pools orbit slowly and shrink as they're harvested, so the safest pool this minute can be on the front line the next.

The Abyss

Radial vignette · the dark rim

The black edge of the seed's background gradient. Nuclei take root here, far from the rich center. In the full game, the Abyss hides Spore beds and stealthy Deep Choir outposts.

Three lights to harvest

Lumen

in demo

Collected from caustic pools by creatures with pili. Pays for hatching and research. Pools regenerate slowly, shrink as they're drained, and drift on slow orbits. The Great Caustic in the center holds twice as much as any other pool.

Silt

in demo

The seed's depth dust. Every nucleus filters it passively, so income never drops to zero and a culture that loses all its foragers can still slowly recover.

Spore

full game

Harvested from micro-plankton, which leave comet trails and are hard to catch. Spore is required for chimeric organs, and it's the Luminants' signature currency.

The Dreamscape has no walls, so the game needs no pathfinding. Open water is a constraint that works in the design's favor: movement is about arcs, pursuit and turn radius, never about chokepoints.
§ 04 · The Six Cultures

Six colors, six ways to live

Each color set in _SWIMMER_COLORS becomes a civilization, and each civilization specializes in one organ line. That means visual identity and mechanical identity are the same thing: you can tell what a culture does from what color it glows.

The Tide Wheel

Each culture pressures two others and is pressured by two. Warm colors are aggressive, cool colors are patient, and violet is the strange one. The loop follows the palette's own emotional logic. Read the table row by row: ▲ means the row culture has the advantage.

§ 05 · Anatomy

A spine, and what grows from it

Every organism is a 20-vertebra spine rebuilt each frame from the path its head has traveled. Organs attach at fixed points on that spine, which the seed's renderers already define.

Legs attach at vertebrae 2, 4, 6 and 8 and stroke in a walking cycle. Flagella extend from the tail as a traveling wave. Pili line the flanks as swaying fronds. Mandibles open and close behind the head. Antennae sweep ahead of it. Each organ attaches to one side, so a second copy of the same organ mirrors to the other side.

The seed generates ten variants per organ. Each step adds length, segment count, amplitude and stroke weight. The game groups them into four research tiers using variants 0, 3, 6 and 9. The in-between variants are reserved for the full game as strains, small mutations a culture drifts into through use.

Five organ lines with four tiers each. Every cell below renders that exact organ at that exact variant using the seed's own render(). Research doesn't just add a number; it visibly grows every creature you own.

What you see in each state: Calm cultures glow in their base color and swim lazily. Active cultures brighten toward their second tone and speed up. Fevered cultures turn amber and then red, their heads flicker, and their paths jerk; this is the seed's panic code, unchanged. Starving cultures fade to the seed's cold low-battery blue. Blighted cultures pulse red. A glance at the enemy tells you whether they're thriving, overheating or dying.

§ 09 · Fever

Burn brighter, burn out

The seed made creatures panic when the phone got hot. The game makes heat a resource you spend. Fever is one shared value per colony, and every rule that touches it follows from the seed's temperature thresholds.

  • Rises with hatching (+4% each), your creatures fighting (+0.4%/s per creature), and Frenzy, a +30% surge you trigger yourself on a 20-second cooldown.
  • Falls by 3% per second when you leave it alone.
  • Pays out as up to +45% speed, +60% bite and faster hatching, scaled linearly.
  • Costs health above 60%: every creature burns (fever − 0.6) × 6 HP per second. Past that point the colony turns red, which is the seed's 42° threshold.
  • Looks like the seed's panic: sudden turns, flickering heads, and a heat-shifted palette. Your opponent can see your fever too.
Fever is how a losing player takes a risk: a frenzied counter-attack can break a siege, or it can burn out the last of your army. Both outcomes are visible in color before they happen.
§ 10 · Economy & Combat

The tide loop

Light turns into bodies, bodies win more light, and fever sits across the whole loop as a throttle.

Combat by anatomy

There are no attack animations to author. A creature fights by swimming. Once an enemy is inside its sense radius (antennae), it turns and pursues (flagella). Inside its reach (mandible length) it circles the target and bites continuously (mandible tier). It stays alive through HP (legs and body). Because the seed's trail-following steering produces pursuit arcs, a fight looks like a swarm of predators closing on prey.

Nuclei defend themselves with a lance of light against the nearest intruder, so an early rush has to commit rather than poke.

§ 11 · Rival Minds

How a culture thinks

The demo opponent is deliberately simple: a readable state machine that plays to its culture's strengths.

  • Grow. Keep five foragers working (seven for the Verdant Strain), and replace any that die.
  • Evolve. Research the culture's home organ first, then bite, legs, flagella, antennae.
  • Mass. Hatch wardens and signature organisms until the army reaches the current wave size.
  • Surge. Attack-move to the enemy nucleus, trigger Frenzy near the target, and grow the next wave by two.
  • Recoil. If enemies come within 280px of home, pull every warrior back to defend.

Difficulty only changes income: Gentle ×0.8, Tidal ×1.0, Abyssal ×1.3. The rival never cheats on information, and it reads the same colors you do.

§ 13 · Scope & Roadmap

What this proves, and what it doesn't

The demo demonstrates

  • The seed renderer holds up at RTS unit counts (about 60 organisms at once) in plain Canvas 2D.
  • Organs read as roles at a glance. Mandible-heavy swarms and pili-laden foragers are easy to tell apart.
  • Palette-as-HUD works for both players, so you can see a fevered or starving enemy.
  • Drifting pools create shifting fronts with no terrain authoring.
  • Six cultures become mechanically distinct with one rule each.

Deliberately left out

  • Scrolling camera and large maps (the demo is one screen, 1280×720 units)
  • Fog of war (the “Murk”), chimeric organs, Spore, strains
  • Multiplayer, campaigns, audio
  • Unit collision beyond soft separation; formations
  • Balance. Every number here is a first guess.

Risks & open questions

  • Violet vs. purple. The Deep Choir (#7864FF) and the Umbral Kin (#B060FF) sit close together, especially for players with color-vision deficiency. Mitigation: give each culture a distinct nucleus petal count and head glyph, and never let color be the only signal.
  • Gradient cost on mobile. Every head, pool and shimmer is a radial gradient. The seed's lowPowerMode branch already shows how to degrade: drop shimmers and caustics first.
  • Legibility past 100 units. The glow bodies add up. Consider capping wake rendering and using additive blending only for heads.
  • Should body length be HP? It's tempting and very readable, but it couples silhouette to durability in ways that may limit design.
  • Should fever be shared across a colony, or tracked per creature? Shared fever is readable. Per-creature fever allows more tactical play.
